Tutorial

A complete analysis, from file to figure, using the two-arc sample spectrum that ships with EIS Studio. Its true parameters are known, so you can compare your result with the right answer. It takes about ten minutes.

  1. Start EIS Studio and load a spectrum

    Start the app from its shortcut (see Install). In the left panel, either upload your own file or choose synthetic_2arc.csv under “…or try a synthetic sample”. The sample was generated from R0 = 50 Ω, R1 = 200 Ω with a CPE (Q = 1×10⁻⁷, α = 0.9), and R2 = 500 Ω with a CPE (Q = 1×10⁻⁴, α = 0.8), plus 0.3 % noise.

  2. Check how the file was read, and whether the data are consistent

    Under the title, EIS Studio lists which columns it used and anything it converted, such as decimal commas or a −Z″ column. The Data & KK check tab shows the spectrum and the Lin-KK test. Residuals at the level of your instrument's noise, without a trend, mean the spectrum can be fitted meaningfully. A trend at low frequency usually means the cell drifted during the sweep.

    Use Frequency window in the left panel to drop unreliable points, such as cable inductance at the top end or a drifting low-frequency tail.

  3. Enter your sample's dimensions

    Give the thickness and the diameter (or the electrode area), with their measurement uncertainties if you know them. They are used for the conductivity and, if you choose, for area-specific (Ω cm²) or resistivity (Ω cm) axes on every figure.

  4. Choose a circuit and fit it

    In Equivalent circuit (ECM), pick a preset or type a circuit: - joins elements in series and p(a,b) in parallel. For this sample, choose “Two arcs”. The table fills with starting values estimated from the data; edit them, tick fixed to hold a parameter, or narrow its bounds. Then press Fit circuit.

    Multi-start fits repeats the fit from several random starting points and keeps the best. If equally good fits disagree about a parameter, EIS Studio tells you that the data do not determine it. Take that warning seriously before reporting the value.

  5. Read the fit

    Check the fit quality line first: residuals near the noise level and the best minimum reached by most starting points. Then the parameters: R1 = 199.5 Ω and R2 = 500.2 Ω, against the true 200 and 500 Ω. For each R–CPE pair you also get the effective capacitance, which helps assign an arc to the bulk, grain boundaries or the electrode, and its characteristic frequency.

    Under Conductivity, choose which resistances make up the ionic resistance: the bulk alone, or bulk plus grain boundary for the total conductivity.

    Solid electrolytes at room temperature. For fast ion conductors (mS cm⁻¹) the bulk arc is usually above the highest frequency your potentiostat measures. Then only the sum R0 + R1 is determined, and that sum is the resistance to use for the conductivity. EIS Studio warns when an arc's characteristic frequency lies outside your data.

  6. Compute the DRT

    The DRT tab finds the processes in your spectrum without assuming a circuit; it's a good way to decide how many elements your circuit needs. The defaults (Gaussian basis, combined Re–Im data, λ by generalised cross-validation) suit most spectra. Tick Bayesian credible band to see how well each part of γ(τ) is determined.

    Blocking electrodes. A capacitive tail has no finite DC resistance, which breaks the DRT model. Set f min above the start of the tail before computing the DRT; EIS Studio warns you if you forget.

  7. Export figures and data

    Set the figure width (single or double journal column), units, font size and format in Figures in the left panel. Each analysis then offers its composite figure, every single panel, CSV tables and a ZIP with everything in PNG, PDF and SVG plus a settings file.

Several measurements at once: activation energy and electronic conductivity

Series mode analyses spectra at several temperatures (or of several pellets) and DC polarisation steps together. They can come from one export, such as an EC-Lab linked experiment, from several files, or both. The sample set used here is one pellet (1.00 mm × 10.0 mm) simulated at 25–85 °C with Ea = 0.45 eV and σ(25 °C) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ S cm⁻¹, followed by five potential steps for an electronic conductivity of 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ S cm⁻¹.

  1. Load the files and enter the temperatures

    In the left panel choose Series (several measurements), then upload your files or choose the sample set “Temperature series + DC steps”. EIS Studio lists every spectrum and potentiostatic step it found. Type the temperatures in order into the quick-entry box and press Fill, or edit the table directly. For pellets of different size or density, give each its own thickness, diameter and mass in the table.

  2. Set up the analysis

    Choose how the ionic resistance is taken from each spectrum: an equivalent-circuit fit, Z′ at the minimum of −Z″ between the arc and the blocking spike, or the spike extrapolated to the real axis. For a fit, the circuit editor, presets, parameter table and fit options are the same as in single-spectrum mode, and they apply to every spectrum: fixed values and bounds hold for all of them, and the starting values are either guessed from each spectrum's own arcs (the default), taken from the table, or carried over from the previous, colder spectrum. Pick the resistances that make up the ionic resistance (e.g. bulk only, or bulk + grain boundary).

    The DRT settings below it are those of single-spectrum mode as well. In series mode λ is manual by default, so every spectrum is regularised the same way and the DRTs can be compared. A blocking-electrode tail has no finite resistance, so the points given to the DRT stop at low frequency: where −phase first rises above θ after the arcs (default), at the −Z″ minimum (most conservative), or not at all. Press Analyse spectra.

  3. Read the results

    You get R and σ for every spectrum, the worst fit and Kramers–Kronig residuals, and from the Arrhenius fit the activation energy with its standard error and σ at 25 °C.

    Below them, every fitted parameter and the DRT peaks of every spectrum are tabulated, and any parameter can be plotted against temperature.

    Check the assignment at every temperature. As a pellet warms, its bulk arc moves out of the window and the intercept starts to include the grain boundaries; a fit may then split the resistance differently between elements. An Arrhenius line that mixes “bulk” at low temperature with “total” at high temperature bends and gives a wrong Ea. The effective capacitance of a bulk arc should change little with temperature: plot it.

  4. Compare the spectra

    Nyquist, Bode |Z|, −phase and DRT are overlaid, coloured from blue (coldest) to orange (hottest). Blocking spikes are hidden from the Nyquist plot so the arcs stay visible. Each DRT is drawn dotted beyond the lowest frequency it was given, and the DRT axis is scaled to the arcs, so peaks made by the onset of the tail run off the top. Normalise impedance scales every spectrum by its own geometry (Ω cm), by geometry and relative density, or by its own resistance to compare shapes only.

  5. Inspect one spectrum

    Pick a spectrum to see its circuit fit, DRT and Kramers–Kronig check with the same figures and downloads as in single-spectrum mode. A spectrum the series settings don't suit can be refitted with its own parameter table, and its DRT can be given its own low-frequency limit. Moving that limit is also the test for a peak at the long-τ end: a real process stays where it is, a peak made by the tail follows the cut.

  6. Electronic conductivity from the DC steps

    The DC polarisation tab averages the current over the last part of each step, checks that it has stopped changing, and fits the steady-state currents against potential. With ion-blocking electrodes the slope gives Re and σe = L/(Re·A). Choose a spectrum of the same pellet to get the electronic transference number.

With your own data

  • Analyse one sweep at a time. Files with several cycles get a Sweep selector in the left panel.
  • If the Nyquist plot is dominated by a blocking-electrode tail, use Nyquist zoom under Figures to show the arcs.
  • Keep the settings file from each ZIP with your raw data; it records exactly how the analysis was done.
  • For the equations and conventions behind every number, see Methods.
