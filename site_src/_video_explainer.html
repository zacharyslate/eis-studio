Transcript of the captions

How a spectrum is built

Impedance spectroscopy applies a small sine-wave voltage V and measures the current I (top right). Z = V/I has a size |Z| and a phase: how far the current is shifted in time against the voltage.

The measurement is repeated from high to low frequency, one point per frequency. −Z″ against Z′ gives the Nyquist plot; the phase against frequency gives the Bode plot.

Each process has a time constant τ = RC and responds most at f = 1/(2πτ): its point tops the arc and the DRT peaks there, with area R. The phase peak sits nearby, shifted by the series resistance.

At high frequency the interface charges and discharges so fast that its capacitance passes the current: only the electrolyte resistance R₀ is felt. The point sits on the real axis.

Slower now: part of the current charges the double layer (C₁), part drives the reaction across the interface (R₁). The current leads the voltage and the point climbs the arc.

One process, one arc: R₀ is where the arc starts, R₁ is its width, and its top lies at f = 1/(2πR₁C₁) ≈ 160 Hz. Real cells have several processes in series, each with its own τ. Here are four.

A solid electrolyte pellet

A pellet pressed from a solid electrolyte powder sits between two metal electrodes that conduct electrons but block ions. We sweep from 10 MHz down to 20 Hz.

Here both arcs fit in the window: a slower conductor, or a cooled sample. For fast sulfides at room temperature they lie above 10 MHz; only the total resistance is seen as the intercept.

Highest frequencies: Li⁺ hop back and forth between sites inside the grains. The bulk resistance and the tiny geometric capacitance (pF) give the first, fastest arc.

Lower frequencies: the grain boundaries respond. They hinder the ions (R₂) but are very thin, so their capacitance is larger (nF) and their arc appears at lower frequency.

Lowest frequencies: the ions reach the electrodes but cannot cross them. They pile up in a double layer (µF), which behaves like a capacitor: a steep spike, no closing arc.

Arc widths give the bulk (R₁) and grain-boundary (R₂) resistances; σ = L/(R·A) with R₁ (bulk) or R₁ + R₂ (total). The spike shows the carriers are ions; the DRT leaves it out.

An all-solid-state battery

A full cell: lithium metal anode, a solid-electrolyte separator and a composite cathode of active particles mixed with solid electrolyte. We sweep from 1 MHz down to 5 mHz.

Each layer and interface responds at its own speed, so the spectrum separates them.

Highest frequencies: only ion transport through the solid-electrolyte separator responds. Its bulk and grain-boundary arcs lie above 1 MHz, so it appears as the intercept R₀.

Around 1 kHz: Li⁺ cross into the lithium metal. Voids and poor contact squeeze the current through small spots and enlarge this arc; stack pressure shrinks it. Reaction layers add to it.

A few Hz: charge transfer between the solid electrolyte and the cathode particles, in parallel with their double layer. In sulfide cells it is often the largest arc.

Below about 1 Hz: Li diffuses into and out of the cathode particles. The slower the signal, the deeper the concentration wave reaches: the 45° Warburg line, turning vertical once the whole particle follows the signal.

Read off: separator resistance R₀, interface resistances R₁ and R₂, and diffusion. The spectrum alone does not say which interface is which: symmetric cells, temperature and SOC do.

A PEM water electrolyzer

Water is split while a DC current flows; a small AC signal rides on top of it. The impedance belongs to that operating point and is given per cm² of cell.

Here a low current (about 0.1 A cm⁻²), where the reaction arcs are large. At 1–2 A cm⁻² they shrink and the membrane dominates.

Highest frequencies: protons move through the membrane (and electrons through contacts and plates). Purely resistive: the high-frequency intercept, the ohmic loss.

A few kHz: usually assigned to the cathode, where H₂ evolves on platinum. The reaction is fast, so the arc is small; part of it may come from the ionomer or contacts.

Tens of Hz: the oxygen evolution reaction on the iridium-oxide anode. It is much slower, and its arc dominates the kinetic losses.

Around 1 Hz: often assigned to mass transport, water in and gas out through the porous layers. Bubbles block part of the surface; this arc grows at high current.

Read off: the ohmic loss, the kinetic loss of each electrode and the mass-transport loss. Their sum equals the slope of the polarization curve at this current. Low-frequency assignments are debated; spectra at several currents help.

A lithium-ion battery

A commercial-style cell, measured at rest. The impedance is only tens of milliohms, so cables and wiring matter. We sweep from 10 kHz down to 5 mHz.

Graphite anode, porous separator soaked in liquid electrolyte, NMC cathode.

Highest frequencies: the cable and the cell's wound electrodes act as an inductor. The points fall below the axis. This is the wiring, not chemistry.

Where the curve crosses the axis: the ohmic resistance of the liquid electrolyte in the separator and pores, plus current collectors and contacts.

Around 1 kHz: often assigned to Li⁺ crossing the SEI film on graphite, which grows with ageing. Contact resistance to the current collector gives an arc here too.

Tens of Hz: Li⁺ cross the particle surfaces of both electrodes (charge transfer) while the double layer charges. It depends strongly on temperature and state of charge.

Below about 1 Hz: Li diffuses inside the particles: the 45° line. Far below this sweep the particles would fill uniformly and the line would turn vertical, like a capacitor.

Read off: the ohmic resistance, the high-frequency and charge-transfer arcs, and diffusion. Tracking how they grow as a cell ages is the basis of impedance-based diagnostics.
