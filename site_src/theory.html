The measurement

An impedance analyser applies a small sinusoidal voltage to the sample and records the current that flows. Comparing the two waves at one frequency gives two numbers: how large the current is, and how far it is shifted in time. Repeating this over many frequencies, typically from megahertz down to millihertz, gives the impedance spectrum.

\[ V(t) = V_0 \sin(\omega t), \qquad I(t) = I_0 \sin(\omega t + \varphi), \qquad \omega = 2\pi f \]

The impedance is the ratio of the two waves, written as a complex number so it carries both the amplitude ratio and the phase:

\[ Z(\omega) = \frac{\tilde{V}}{\tilde{I}} = \left|Z\right| e^{j\varphi_Z} = Z' + jZ'' \]
\[ \left|Z\right| = \frac{V_0}{I_0} = \sqrt{{Z'}^2 + {Z''}^2}, \qquad \varphi_Z = \arctan\frac{Z''}{Z'} = -\varphi \]

Z′ is the real (resistive) part and Z″ the imaginary (reactive) part. For a capacitive sample Z″ is negative, so plots show −Z″ to keep the arcs above the axis. Two plots are standard. The Nyquist plot shows −Z″ against Z′ (each point is one frequency, and both axes must have the same scale or arcs look distorted). The Bode plot shows |Z| and the phase against frequency.

Two conditions make the analysis valid. The response must be linear, so the amplitude is kept small (commonly a few to a few tens of millivolts), and the sample must be stable while the sweep runs. A Kramers–Kronig test, such as the Lin-KK check in EIS Studio, tests both after the fact.

Circuit elements

A spectrum is modelled with a few ideal elements whose impedances are known exactly. They combine like resistors in ordinary circuits, but with complex numbers.

The RC arc

A resistor in parallel with a capacitor is the basic unit of most spectra. Adding a series resistance R₀ (wires, contacts, or a process too fast to resolve) gives

\[ Z = R_0 + \frac{R_1}{1 + j\omega\tau}, \qquad \tau = R_1 C_1 \]
\[ Z' = R_0 + \frac{R_1}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}, \qquad -Z'' = \frac{\omega\tau R_1}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2} \]

The top of the arc is where the real and imaginary parts of the RC term are equal, which gives the characteristic frequency and the time constant:

\[ f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi R_1 C_1} = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau}, \qquad -{Z''}_{\max} = \frac{R_1}{2} \]
\[ f_{\varphi,\max} = f_c\sqrt{1 + R_1/R_0} \]

Depressed arcs and the constant phase element

Real arcs are usually flattened: their centre lies below the real axis. This comes from a spread of time constants (rough electrodes, grain-size distributions, inhomogeneous current), and it is modelled by replacing the capacitor with a CPE:

\[ Z_{R\parallel\mathrm{CPE}} = \frac{R}{1 + RQ\,(j\omega)^{\alpha}}, \qquad \omega_c = (RQ)^{-1/\alpha} \]

Q is not a capacitance: its unit is F sα−1, so it can't be compared with literature capacitances directly. Convert it to an effective capacitance first (Hsu & Mansfeld, 2001):

\[ C_\mathrm{eff} = Q^{1/\alpha}\,R^{(1-\alpha)/\alpha} = \frac{\tau}{R}, \qquad \tau = (RQ)^{1/\alpha} \]

Worked example

A fit gives R = 200 Ω, Q = 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ F sα−1 and α = 0.90.

\[ \tau = (200 \times 1.00\times10^{-7})^{1/0.9} = 6.01\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s} \]
\[ C_\mathrm{eff} = \frac{6.01\times10^{-6}}{200} = 3.01\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{F}, \qquad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau} = 26.5\ \mathrm{kHz} \]

Reading the spectrum of a solid electrolyte pellet

To measure ionic conductivity, the pellet sits between two ion-blocking electrodes: metals or carbon that conduct electrons but do not exchange ions with the electrolyte. Ions move freely through the pellet but pile up at the electrodes, which then behave like capacitors.

A polycrystalline pellet then shows up to three features, from high to low frequency: transport through the grains (bulk), transport across grain boundaries, and the blocking electrodes. Each RC process can be identified by its capacitance, which reflects the thickness of the layer being charged.

Why the bulk arc of a fast conductor is never seen

The bulk resistance and the bulk capacitance both depend on the geometry, but their product does not. The geometry cancels, and the bulk arc's frequency depends only on the material:

\[ \tau_b = R_b C_b = \frac{L}{\sigma A}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{L} = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\sigma}, \qquad f_b = \frac{\sigma}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r} \]

Here ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹ is the vacuum permittivity, εr is the relative permittivity of the electrolyte, and σ is in S m⁻¹ (1 mS cm⁻¹ = 0.1 S m⁻¹).

For such samples the bulk (or bulk + grain-boundary) resistance is the high-frequency intercept of the spectrum with the real axis: the point where the electrode spike starts. In a circuit fit it is the series resistance R₀. Fitting an extra RC element for an arc you didn't measure gives numbers the data do not determine; EIS Studio warns when that happens.

Ionic or electronic?

Ion-blocking electrodes still pass electrons. A purely ionic conductor therefore gives a spike that rises without limit at low frequency. If the sample also conducts electrons, the spike bends over into an arc that closes on the real axis at the electronic resistance. Quantify a small electronic contribution with a separate DC polarisation measurement (see Huggins, 2002).

Calculating the ionic conductivity

Resistance depends on the sample's shape; conductivity is the material property. For a pellet of thickness L with electrodes of area A:

\[ \sigma = \frac{L}{R\,A} = \frac{K}{R}, \qquad K = \frac{L}{A}, \qquad A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} \]

With L in cm, A in cm² and R in Ω, σ comes out in S cm⁻¹. K is the cell constant. R is the ionic resistance you choose: the bulk alone for the grain conductivity, or bulk plus grain boundary for the total conductivity. Say which one you report. The area-specific resistance, used for electrolytes in a cell stack, is

\[ \mathrm{ASR} = R\,A \quad (\Omega\ \mathrm{cm}^2) \]

Uncertainty

Independent relative errors add in quadrature. For a disc, the area error is twice the diameter error:

\[ \left(\frac{\delta\sigma}{\sigma}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\delta L}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta R}{R}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta A}{A}\right)^2, \qquad \frac{\delta A}{A} = 2\,\frac{\delta d}{d} \]

Worked example

L = 1.00 ± 0.01 mm, d = 10.00 ± 0.05 mm, and the fitted ionic resistance is R = 50.0 ± 0.5 Ω.

\[ A = \frac{\pi\,(1.000\ \mathrm{cm})^2}{4} = 0.7854\ \mathrm{cm}^2, \qquad K = \frac{0.100\ \mathrm{cm}}{0.7854\ \mathrm{cm}^2} = 0.1273\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1} \]
\[ \sigma = \frac{0.1273\ \mathrm{cm}^{-1}}{50.0\ \Omega} = 2.546\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{S\,cm^{-1}} \qquad = 2.55\ \mathrm{mS\,cm^{-1}} \]
\[ \frac{\delta\sigma}{\sigma} = \sqrt{(0.010)^2 + (0.010)^2 + (2\times0.005)^2} = 0.0173 \qquad \Rightarrow\; \sigma = 2.546 \pm 0.044\ \mathrm{mS\,cm^{-1}} \]

The area-specific resistance is 50.0 Ω × 0.7854 cm² = 39.3 Ω cm². Each of the three sources contributes equally here. A thickness gauge good to ±0.01 mm on a 1 mm pellet already limits you to 1 %, so a thinner pellet needs a better gauge.

Before you report it

  • Density. Pores lower the measured value. Report the relative density of the pellet with the conductivity.
  • Area. Use the electrode contact area. If the electrodes are smaller than the pellet, the pellet's own diameter overestimates A.
  • Thickness. Measure it as it was during the measurement. Pellets pressed in the cell can be thinner under load than when released.
  • Pressure and temperature. Soft electrolytes such as sulfides are often measured under stack pressure, which changes the result. Report pressure and temperature.

Temperature dependence and activation energy

Ion hopping is thermally activated, so conductivity rises steeply with temperature. Measuring σ at several temperatures and plotting ln(σT) against 1/T gives a straight line whose slope is the activation energy:

\[ \sigma T = A\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{k_B T}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ln(\sigma T) = \ln A - \frac{E_a}{k_B}\cdot\frac{1}{T} \]
\[ E_a = -k_B\,\frac{d\ln(\sigma T)}{d(1/T)}, \qquad k_B = 8.617\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV\,K^{-1}}, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{eV} = 96.49\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}} \]

Many papers instead fit σ = σ₀ exp(−Ea/kBT), without the factor T. Both forms are common and they give different numbers from the same data, so always state which one you used.

Worked example: two temperatures

σ = 2.5 mS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C (298.15 K) and 6.0 mS cm⁻¹ at 60 °C (333.15 K).

\[ E_a = k_B\,\frac{\ln\!\big(\sigma_2 T_2 / \sigma_1 T_1\big)}{1/T_1 - 1/T_2} = 8.617\times10^{-5}\times\frac{\ln(1998.9/745.4)}{3.524\times10^{-4}} = 0.241\ \mathrm{eV} \]

That is 23.3 kJ mol⁻¹. Without the factor T the same two points give 0.214 eV, a difference of about 0.03 eV from the choice of form alone. Two points also give no uncertainty; use at least four or five temperatures and the regression below.

The distribution of relaxation times

An equivalent circuit has to be chosen before fitting. The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) avoids that choice: it describes the spectrum as a continuous sum of RC elements and solves for how much resistance sits at each time constant.

\[ Z(f) = R_\infty + j2\pi f L + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\gamma(\ln\tau)}{1 + j2\pi f\tau}\, d\ln\tau \]
\[ R_k = \int_{\text{peak } k} \gamma(\ln\tau)\, d\ln\tau, \qquad f_k = \frac{1}{2\pi\tau_k} \]

Recovering γ from a noisy spectrum is ill-posed, so it is regularised. The result depends on the regularisation parameter λ, and it fails for a blocking-electrode spike, which has no finite DC resistance: crop the spike before computing a DRT.
