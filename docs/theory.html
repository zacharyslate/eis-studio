How a spectrum is built

Impedance spectroscopy applies a small sine-wave voltage V and measures the current I (top right). Z = V/I has a size |Z| and a phase: how far the current is shifted in time against the voltage.

The measurement is repeated from high to low frequency, one point per frequency. −Z″ against Z′ gives the Nyquist plot; the phase against frequency gives the Bode plot.

Each process has a time constant τ = RC and responds most at f = 1/(2πτ): its point tops the arc and the DRT peaks there, with area R. The phase peak sits nearby, shifted by the series resistance.

At high frequency the interface charges and discharges so fast that its capacitance passes the current: only the electrolyte resistance R₀ is felt. The point sits on the real axis.

Slower now: part of the current charges the double layer (C₁), part drives the reaction across the interface (R₁). The current leads the voltage and the point climbs the arc.

One process, one arc: R₀ is where the arc starts, R₁ is its width, and its top lies at f = 1/(2πR₁C₁) ≈ 160 Hz. Real cells have several processes in series, each with its own τ. Here are four.

A solid electrolyte pellet

A pellet pressed from a solid electrolyte powder sits between two metal electrodes that conduct electrons but block ions. We sweep from 10 MHz down to 20 Hz.

Here both arcs fit in the window: a slower conductor, or a cooled sample. For fast sulfides at room temperature they lie above 10 MHz; only the total resistance is seen as the intercept.

Highest frequencies: Li⁺ hop back and forth between sites inside the grains. The bulk resistance and the tiny geometric capacitance (pF) give the first, fastest arc.

Lower frequencies: the grain boundaries respond. They hinder the ions (R₂) but are very thin, so their capacitance is larger (nF) and their arc appears at lower frequency.

Lowest frequencies: the ions reach the electrodes but cannot cross them. They pile up in a double layer (µF), which behaves like a capacitor: a steep spike, no closing arc.

Arc widths give the bulk (R₁) and grain-boundary (R₂) resistances; σ = L/(R·A) with R₁ (bulk) or R₁ + R₂ (total). The spike shows the carriers are ions; the DRT leaves it out.

An all-solid-state battery

A full cell: lithium metal anode, a solid-electrolyte separator and a composite cathode of active particles mixed with solid electrolyte. We sweep from 1 MHz down to 5 mHz.

Each layer and interface responds at its own speed, so the spectrum separates them.

Highest frequencies: only ion transport through the solid-electrolyte separator responds. Its bulk and grain-boundary arcs lie above 1 MHz, so it appears as the intercept R₀.

Around 1 kHz: Li⁺ cross into the lithium metal. Voids and poor contact squeeze the current through small spots and enlarge this arc; stack pressure shrinks it. Reaction layers add to it.

A few Hz: charge transfer between the solid electrolyte and the cathode particles, in parallel with their double layer. In sulfide cells it is often the largest arc.

Below about 1 Hz: Li diffuses into and out of the cathode particles. The slower the signal, the deeper the concentration wave reaches: the 45° Warburg line, turning vertical once the whole particle follows the signal.

Read off: separator resistance R₀, interface resistances R₁ and R₂, and diffusion. The spectrum alone does not say which interface is which: symmetric cells, temperature and SOC do.

A PEM water electrolyzer

Water is split while a DC current flows; a small AC signal rides on top of it. The impedance belongs to that operating point and is given per cm² of cell.

Here a low current (about 0.1 A cm⁻²), where the reaction arcs are large. At 1–2 A cm⁻² they shrink and the membrane dominates.

Highest frequencies: protons move through the membrane (and electrons through contacts and plates). Purely resistive: the high-frequency intercept, the ohmic loss.

A few kHz: usually assigned to the cathode, where H₂ evolves on platinum. The reaction is fast, so the arc is small; part of it may come from the ionomer or contacts.

Tens of Hz: the oxygen evolution reaction on the iridium-oxide anode. It is much slower, and its arc dominates the kinetic losses.

Around 1 Hz: often assigned to mass transport, water in and gas out through the porous layers. Bubbles block part of the surface; this arc grows at high current.

Read off: the ohmic loss, the kinetic loss of each electrode and the mass-transport loss. Their sum equals the slope of the polarization curve at this current. Low-frequency assignments are debated; spectra at several currents help.

A lithium-ion battery

A commercial-style cell, measured at rest. The impedance is only tens of milliohms, so cables and wiring matter. We sweep from 10 kHz down to 5 mHz.

Graphite anode, porous separator soaked in liquid electrolyte, NMC cathode.

Highest frequencies: the cable and the cell's wound electrodes act as an inductor. The points fall below the axis. This is the wiring, not chemistry.

Where the curve crosses the axis: the ohmic resistance of the liquid electrolyte in the separator and pores, plus current collectors and contacts.

Around 1 kHz: often assigned to Li⁺ crossing the SEI film on graphite, which grows with ageing. Contact resistance to the current collector gives an arc here too.

Tens of Hz: Li⁺ cross the particle surfaces of both electrodes (charge transfer) while the double layer charges. It depends strongly on temperature and state of charge.

Below about 1 Hz: Li diffuses inside the particles: the 45° line. Far below this sweep the particles would fill uniformly and the line would turn vertical, like a capacitor.

Read off: the ohmic resistance, the high-frequency and charge-transfer arcs, and diffusion. Tracking how they grow as a cell ages is the basis of impedance-based diagnostics.

The measurement

An impedance analyser applies a small sinusoidal voltage to the sample and records the current that flows. Comparing the two waves at one frequency gives two numbers: how large the current is, and how far it is shifted in time. Repeating this over many frequencies, typically from megahertz down to millihertz, gives the impedance spectrum.

V(t)=V0sin(ωt)I(t)=I0sin(ωt+φ)ω=2πf

The impedance is the ratio of the two waves, written as a complex number so it carries both the amplitude ratio and the phase:

Z(ω)=V~I~=|Z|ejφZ=Z′+jZ″
|Z|=V0I0=Z′2+Z″2φZ=arctanZ″Z′=−φ

Z′ is the real (resistive) part and Z″ the imaginary (reactive) part. For a capacitive sample Z″ is negative, so plots show −Z″ to keep the arcs above the axis. Two plots are standard. The Nyquist plot shows −Z″ against Z′ (each point is one frequency, and both axes must have the same scale or arcs look distorted). The Bode plot shows |Z| and the phase against frequency.

Two conditions make the analysis valid. The response must be linear, so the amplitude is kept small (commonly a few to a few tens of millivolts), and the sample must be stable while the sweep runs. A Kramers–Kronig test, such as the Lin-KK check in EIS Studio, tests both after the fact.

The RC arc

A resistor in parallel with a capacitor is the basic unit of most spectra. Adding a series resistance R₀ (wires, contacts, or a process too fast to resolve) gives

Z=R0+R11+jωττ=R1C1
Z′=R0+R11+ω2τ2−Z″=ωτR11+ω2τ2

The top of the arc is where the real and imaginary parts of the RC term are equal, which gives the characteristic frequency and the time constant:

fc=12πR1C1=12πτ−Z″max=R12

Depressed arcs and the constant phase element

Real arcs are usually flattened: their centre lies below the real axis. This comes from a spread of time constants (rough electrodes, grain-size distributions, inhomogeneous current), and it is modelled by replacing the capacitor with a CPE:

ZR∥CPE=R1+RQ(jω)αωc=(RQ)−1/α

Q is not a capacitance: its unit is F sα−1, so it can't be compared with literature capacitances directly. Convert it to an effective capacitance first (Hsu & Mansfeld, 2001):

Ceff=Q1/αR(1−α)/α=τRτ=(RQ)1/α

Worked example

A fit gives R = 200 Ω, Q = 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ F sα−1 and α = 0.90.

τ=(200×1.00×10−7)1/0.9=6.01×10−6 s
Ceff=6.01×10−6200=3.01×10−8 Ffc=12πτ=26.5 kHz

Reading the spectrum of a solid electrolyte pellet

To measure ionic conductivity, the pellet sits between two ion-blocking electrodes: metals or carbon that conduct electrons but do not exchange ions with the electrolyte. Ions move freely through the pellet but pile up at the electrodes, which then behave like capacitors.

A polycrystalline pellet then shows up to three features, from high to low frequency: transport through the grains (bulk), transport across grain boundaries, and the blocking electrodes. Each RC process can be identified by its capacitance, which reflects the thickness of the layer being charged.

Why the bulk arc of a fast conductor is never seen

The bulk resistance and the bulk capacitance both depend on the geometry, but their product does not. The geometry cancels, and the bulk arc's frequency depends only on the material:

τb=RbCb=LσA·ε0εrAL=ε0εrσfb=σ2πε0εr

Here ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹ is the vacuum permittivity, εr is the relative permittivity of the electrolyte, and σ is in S m⁻¹ (1 mS cm⁻¹ = 0.1 S m⁻¹).

For such samples the bulk (or bulk + grain-boundary) resistance is the high-frequency intercept of the spectrum with the real axis: the point where the electrode spike starts. In a circuit fit it is the series resistance R₀. Fitting an extra RC element for an arc you didn't measure gives numbers the data do not determine; EIS Studio warns when that happens.

Ionic or electronic?

Ion-blocking electrodes still pass electrons. A purely ionic conductor therefore gives a spike that rises without limit at low frequency. If the sample also conducts electrons, the spike bends over into an arc that closes on the real axis at the electronic resistance. Quantify a small electronic contribution with a separate DC polarisation measurement (see Huggins, 2002).

Calculating the ionic conductivity

Resistance depends on the sample's shape; conductivity is the material property. For a pellet of thickness L with electrodes of area A:

σ=LRA=KRK=LAA=πd24

With L in cm, A in cm² and R in Ω, σ comes out in S cm⁻¹. K is the cell constant. R is the ionic resistance you choose: the bulk alone for the grain conductivity, or bulk plus grain boundary for the total conductivity. Say which one you report. The area-specific resistance, used for electrolytes in a cell stack, is

ASR=RA(Ω cm2)

Uncertainty

Independent relative errors add in quadrature. For a disc, the area error is twice the diameter error:

(δσσ)2=(δLL)2+(δRR)2+(δAA)2δAA=2δdd

Worked example

L = 1.00 ± 0.01 mm, d = 10.00 ± 0.05 mm, and the fitted ionic resistance is R = 50.0 ± 0.5 Ω.

A=π(1.000 cm)24=0.7854 cm2K=0.100 cm0.7854 cm2=0.1273 cm−1
σ=0.1273 cm−150.0 Ω=2.546×10−3 Scm−1=2.55 mScm−1
δσσ=(0.010)2+(0.010)2+(2×0.005)2=0.0173⇒σ=2.546±0.044 mScm−1

The area-specific resistance is 50.0 Ω × 0.7854 cm² = 39.3 Ω cm². Each of the three sources contributes equally here. A thickness gauge good to ±0.01 mm on a 1 mm pellet already limits you to 1 %, so a thinner pellet needs a better gauge.

Before you report it

  • Density. Pores lower the measured value. Report the relative density of the pellet with the conductivity.
  • Area. Use the electrode contact area. If the electrodes are smaller than the pellet, the pellet's own diameter overestimates A.
  • Thickness. Measure it as it was during the measurement. Pellets pressed in the cell can be thinner under load than when released.
  • Pressure and temperature. Soft electrolytes such as sulfides are often measured under stack pressure, which changes the result. Report pressure and temperature.

Temperature dependence and activation energy

Ion hopping is thermally activated, so conductivity rises steeply with temperature. Measuring σ at several temperatures and plotting ln(σT) against 1/T gives a straight line whose slope is the activation energy:

σT=Aexp(−EakBT)⇒ln(σT)=lnA−EakB·1T
Ea=−kBdln(σT)d(1/T)kB=8.617×10−5 eVK−11 eV=96.49 kJmol−1

Many papers instead fit σ = σ₀ exp(−Ea/kBT), without the factor T. Both forms are common and they give different numbers from the same data, so always state which one you used.

Worked example: two temperatures

σ = 2.5 mS cm⁻¹ at 25 °C (298.15 K) and 6.0 mS cm⁻¹ at 60 °C (333.15 K).

Ea=kBln(σ2T2/σ1T1)1/T1−1/T2=8.617×10−5×ln(1998.9/745.4)3.524×10−4=0.241 eV

That is 23.3 kJ mol⁻¹. Without the factor T the same two points give 0.214 eV, a difference of about 0.03 eV from the choice of form alone. Two points also give no uncertainty; use at least four or five temperatures and the regression below.

The distribution of relaxation times

An equivalent circuit has to be chosen before fitting. The distribution of relaxation times (DRT) avoids that choice: it describes the spectrum as a continuous sum of RC elements and solves for how much resistance sits at each time constant.

Z(f)=R∞+j2πfL+∫−∞∞γ(lnτ)1+j2πfτdlnτ
Rk=∫peak kγ(lnτ)dlnτfk=12πτk

Recovering γ from a noisy spectrum is ill-posed, so it is regularised. The result depends on the regularisation parameter λ, and it fails for a blocking-electrode spike, which has no finite DC resistance: crop the spike before computing a DRT.
